Chapter One — The Village

Born Without Shoes, Destined to Run

Pilavullakandi Thekkeparambil Usha — the world would come to know her simply as P.T. Usha — was born on 27 June 1964 in Payyoli, a small coastal village near Kozhikode (Calicut), Kerala. Her family had little. Poverty was not a distant concept; it was a daily reality. Illness visited often. A future in international athletics was not just unlikely — it was unimaginable.

Yet even in childhood, something set Usha apart. At just nine years old, at a school race in fourth grade, she effortlessly overtook the school champion — a girl three years her senior. Teachers noticed. Parents noticed. Usha just kept running.

A scholarship of just ₹250 from the Kerala government was the small nudge that changed everything. It brought her to a special sports school in Kannur (Cannanore) — and into the orbit of the man who would shape her destiny.

Chapter Two — The Mentor

O.M. Nambiar and the Making of a Champion

At the 1979 National School Games, a coach named O.M. Nambiar spotted a teenage girl from Payyoli with an extraordinary stride. He saw not just speed — he saw potential that could, with the right guidance, rival the best in Asia.

Nambiar became Usha's lifelong mentor. Under his coaching, she transformed raw talent into precision athletics. He drilled her in technique, strategy, and mental discipline. The relationship between athlete and coach became one of the most celebrated in Indian sports history.

"A great athlete needs a great coach — but a great coach also needs an athlete brave enough to push beyond every limit they've ever known."

— On the Usha–Nambiar partnership
Chapter Three — The Rise

Dominating Asia, Electrifying India

Usha made her international debut at the 1980 Moscow Olympics, stepping onto the world stage as a teenager. But it was the 1982 Asian Games in New Delhi where the nation truly took notice — she won silver in both the 100m and 200m events.

Then came her golden era. At the 1985 Asian Championships in Jakarta, she reached a performance level that left the continent breathless — winning five gold medals in the 100m, 200m, 400m, 400m hurdles, and 4×400m relay. She earned the title that would follow her forever: Sprint Queen of Asia.

At the 1986 Seoul Asian Games, she claimed four golds and a silver, and was awarded the prestigious Adidas Golden Shoe for the best athlete of the games. Between 1983 and 1989, she accumulated 13 gold medals in Asian championships alone — a record that made her, statistically, the most successful athlete India had ever produced on the track.

The 1/100th of a Second That Broke a Nation

No moment in Indian athletics is more painfully remembered — or more powerfully motivating — than the 400m hurdles final at the 1984 Los Angeles Olympics.

Usha ran the race of her life. She crossed the finish line with everything she had given. When the electronic timers settled, she had finished fourth. The bronze medal — the first-ever Olympic athletics medal for India — went to the Romanian athlete who edged her out by 0.01 seconds. One hundredth of a second.

A nation wept. And yet — this is the part that matters most — Usha did not quit. She went back to the track. She trained harder. She won more. She built a school so the next generation of Indian athletes would never have to face those odds alone.

That 0.01 seconds is not the story of a defeat. It is the story of a woman who refused to let a fraction of time define her life.

Chapter Four — The Legacy

From Track to Nation Builder

After retiring from competitive athletics in 1990, P.T. Usha channelled her energy into a mission: ensuring the next generation of Indian women would have the support she never did.

In 2002, she founded the Usha School of Athletics in Koyilandy (Kinalur), Kozhikode, Kerala — a training academy for young women from low-income families. The school has produced national champions. Its most celebrated alumna, Tintu Luka, holds India's 800m national record and won gold at the Asian Games.

Awards & Recognitions

Honoured by a Grateful Nation

India's highest sporting and civilian honours recognised what millions already knew. Usha received the Arjuna Award (1984) and Padma Shri (1985). In 1985, she was named best women's athlete at the Jakarta Asian Athletics Meet.

In July 2022, the Government of India nominated her to the Rajya Sabha, India's upper house of Parliament — the first Olympian ever to be elected as President of the Indian Olympic Association (IOA). The girl from Payyoli had run all the way to the highest halls of power.

What We Can Learn

Five Lessons from the Payyoli Express

Every young person who reads Usha's story will take something different from it. But these five lessons ring clearest through the decades:

1
Your starting point is not your finishing point. Usha came from poverty, illness, and a village most people had never heard of. None of it stopped her.
2
Failure is not the end of the story. The 0.01-second loss at the 1984 Olympics could have ended her career. Instead, she came back stronger.
3
One good mentor can change everything. O.M. Nambiar saw potential others overlooked. Find your Nambiar — and be a Nambiar for someone else.
4
Discipline compounds over time. She dominated Asian athletics not for one year, but for over a decade. Consistency built that legacy.
5
Legacy is what you give back. Her school has produced champions who would not otherwise have had a chance. That may outlast every medal.
